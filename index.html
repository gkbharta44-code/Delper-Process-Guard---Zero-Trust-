1. Vision and strategic pivot

Delper Guard has moved from a consumer USB endpoint key to a ruggedized, DIN-rail inline industrial firewall and transaction security coprocessor.

Instead of relying on patchable host software, it builds its controls into the silicon. The design targets SL-4, the level meant to resist attackers with substantial resources and specialist skills. It addresses four failure modes specific to autonomous factories:

Telemetry injection
Blocks artificial parameters fed into unencrypted IIoT sensors that would blind anomaly detection.
Digital twin hijacking
Stops lateral moves from compromised IT systems into operational virtual replicas.
Kinematic tampering
Rejects microcode or G-code changes that make machines produce parts with silent structural defects.
Autonomy ransomware cascades
Ensures network deadlocks or account changes never halt the production line.

2. Hardware and silicon architecture

Meeting SL-4 component requirements required a full physical redesign, built on 5nm and 28nm process nodes.

Inline packet filtering

Replaces the USB controller and parses L2 and L3 frames at line rate.

Deep packet inspection

Hardware coprocessors decode Modbus, PROFINET, EtherCAT and OPC UA in isolated trenches.

Flashable secure boot

A hardware root of trust accepts signed, rollback-protected updates across a 10-year life.

Physically unclonable functions

On-chip SRAM noise yields authentication keys at startup, with no static secrets in flash.

Enclosure and write protection

The DIN-rail enclosure runs from -40°C to 85°C and offers dual or quad SFP and RJ-45 Gigabit ports. A hardware-isolated switch layer and a mechanical fail-to-wire relay prevent line locks.

A physical SPDT switch sits inline with the gate of a P-channel MOSFET. In Lock mode it grounds the gate and cuts the programming voltage (VPP), so flash cannot be rewritten over the network. Signed patches pass the lock only through a hardware-validated path from the HSM.

3. Secure development and threat model

The development process is designed to be auditable under IEC 62443-4-1. Every commit to the Verilog parser or HSM firmware passes an automated CI/CD gate and must carry a signature tied to a verified developer hardware token. Firmware follows MISRA C:2012, and the HDL must pass linting for timing race hazards.

The device must withstand more than 5,000,000 malformed Modbus, PROFINET and EtherCAT frames per hour without memory leaks, lockups or relay drops. A dedicated PSIRT must sign and distribute a rollback-protected patch within 72 hours of triage.

5. Autonomous triage and self-healing

Human response is removed from the critical path. A hardware-isolated routine runs quantized ML models, scaling from 0.1M parameters on edge IoT to 100M parameters on the enterprise 5nm tier, which uses an external DRAM interface.

Suspicious packets and unauthorized commands are intercepted in real time and the affected line is isolated. Activity is written to a sealed Quarantine Vault instead of being erased, which keeps the audit trail intact for FIPS 140-3 and PCI-DSS verification. When host software or drivers drift from normal behavior, pre-approved encrypted fixes are applied from a secure whitelist, so transaction processing continues.

6. Roadmap and compliance schedule

  1. Years 1 to 2Launch and certification. Finalize GDSII layouts for the Samsung eFuse architecture, pass independent firmware audits, and complete FIPS 140-3 and ISASecure IEC 62443 testing.
  2. Years 3 to 5AI assurance. Add signed data provenance for model outputs and automated logging validation inside Quarantine Vaults.
  3. Years 5 to 7Post-quantum migration. Move signing and key exchange to Kyber and Dilithium through rollback-protected updates, recertifying each version.
  4. Years 8 to 10Sustained lifecycle. Ongoing threat definition updates and support, with end-of-life notice at least 24 months before the final update.
